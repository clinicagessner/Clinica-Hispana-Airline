# Flu Shot This Fall: Who Needs It, When, and Common Questions

**The flu shot is the one vaccine worth repeating every fall, and almost everyone has a question or two before getting it.** Will it make me sick? I got one last year, isn't that enough? Shouldn't I wait until December? Below are straight answers, so you can decide based on facts rather than on what a coworker or the family group chat says.

At Clínica Hispana Airline we give the [flu vaccine](/services/vacunas) walk-in, administered by medical staff, with everything explained in Spanish or English.

## Is it for you?

The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/flu/vaccines/index.html) recommends a flu vaccine every season for nearly everyone six months and older. Put another way, the real question usually isn't "do I need it?" but "is there any reason I shouldn't get it?" And that reason is uncommon.

Within that "nearly everyone," some people are more likely to have the flu turn serious, so the shot matters even more for them:

- **Adults 65 and older.** Immune defenses against influenza weaken with age, and pneumonia becomes more common.
- **Pregnant women.** The CDC recommends it in any trimester; it protects the mother and also the baby during the first months of life.
- **People with chronic conditions:** diabetes, asthma or COPD, heart problems, kidney disease or a weakened immune system. If you already see us for [diabetes, blood pressure or cholesterol care](/services/condiciones-cronicas), get it at that same visit.
- **Anyone living with babies or older adults.** You might ride out a mild flu and pass it to someone who can't handle it as well.
- **People in public-facing jobs:** retail, restaurants, transportation, cleaning crews, job sites with lots of workers. More contact means more exposure.

If the shot is for your son or daughter, call us before coming in to confirm we give it at their age.

## When to get it: the fall timeline

The vaccine takes **about two weeks** to build protection. The goal is to have it on board before flu starts spreading widely, not once half the block is already sick.

| Timing | What to do |
|---|---|
| September and October | The ideal window according to the CDC. If you can, get it now. |
| November and December | Still worth it. The season is only starting to climb. |
| January onward | Still useful as long as flu is circulating, which often lasts into spring. |

What doesn't work is waiting to "see if it's a bad year." By the time it's obviously a bad year, you've lost those two weeks of head start.

## The questions we hear most

### "The flu shot gives you the flu"

It doesn't. The injectable vaccine contains no live virus that can cause the illness: it uses inactivated virus or just one piece of it. What some people feel afterward is the immune system responding, which is exactly the point.

### "I got vaccinated last year and still got sick"

There are usually three explanations. One: you caught something in the days before the shot or in the two weeks after, before it had kicked in. Two: it wasn't flu but one of the many other respiratory viruses going around in fall and winter, which the flu vaccine doesn't cover. Three: it really was flu, because the vaccine lowers your risk without erasing it. When that happens, the illness tends to be milder, with fewer complications.

### "I already had one last year"

It won't cover you this year. The virus changes, and the vaccine is **reformulated every season** for the strains expected to circulate. Protection also fades over the months. A new dose every fall is the rule.

### "I already had the flu this year"

Getting vaccinated is still a good idea. There are several types of influenza, and having had one doesn't protect you from the others.

### "I'm allergic to eggs"

According to the CDC, an egg allergy no longer rules out the flu vaccine. What you should tell us is whether you've ever had a severe reaction to a previous vaccine.

### "I have a bit of a cold"

A minor illness, like a runny nose, doesn't mean you have to wait. If you have a fever or feel quite unwell, it's better to hold off until you recover. And if what you have looks like real flu, you need something else: the vaccine doesn't treat an infection that has already started (see below).

## Normal side effects and when to worry

What's expected, usually gone in a day or two:

- **A sore arm**, red or slightly swollen where the needle went in
- Fatigue or wanting to turn in early
- Mild body aches or a light headache
- In some people, a low-grade fever

For the arm, keep using it normally (holding it still makes it stiffer) and try a cool cloth. If you take something for the pain, stick with what you already use without trouble.

**Get urgent help or call 911** if, in the minutes or hours afterward, you have trouble breathing, swelling of the face, lips or throat, hives all over your body, severe dizziness or a racing heart. These are rare, serious allergic reactions, and they're not something to wait out at home.

And if your arm gets worse instead of better over the following days, or a fever climbs and won't come down, come in and have the clinic's medical team take a look.

## What to expect from the vaccine (and what not to)

Getting the shot doesn't make you invincible, and it doesn't need to in order to be worth it. What it does:

- Lowers your chances of catching influenza during the season
- Makes the illness tend to be milder if you do catch it
- Reduces the risk of serious complications such as pneumonia or hospitalization, especially for the groups listed above

What it **doesn't** do is cure a flu that's already underway. If you already have fever, body aches and a cough, the useful step is a test: at the clinic we run a [rapid flu and COVID test](/services/enfermedades-respiratorias), with results in minutes. There are antiviral medicines for influenza, and they do the most good if you begin them within the first two days of symptoms.
