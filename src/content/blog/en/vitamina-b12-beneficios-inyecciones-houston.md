# Vitamin B12: When It Is Actually Low and How to Know

**Vitamin B12 is measured with a blood test, and it is worth measuring before you start injecting it.** The test is inexpensive, walk-in, and it keeps you from spending months treating something that was never B12.

Fatigue has many causes. B12 is one. Thyroid is another. Iron-deficiency anemia is another. They look a lot alike, and only lab work separates them.

## What does B12 do?

According to the [U.S. National Institutes of Health](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-Consumer/), B12 is required to form red blood cells, for DNA synthesis, and for normal nervous system function.

Your body does not make it. It comes from diet — meat, fish, eggs, dairy and fortified foods — and is absorbed in the intestine with the help of a stomach protein. When that absorption fails, eating more does not fix anything, and that is where injections make sense.

## What are the symptoms of deficiency?

The ones the NIH describes:

- Fatigue and weakness that rest does not improve
- **Tingling or numbness** in hands and feet
- Balance problems
- A sore tongue or mouth ulcers
- Trouble concentrating, memory lapses
- Megaloblastic anemia, visible on a complete blood count

The tingling is the most specific. Fatigue is the least — twenty different things cause it.

## Who is at higher risk?

- **Adults over 50.** Absorption drops with age even on a good diet.
- **Vegetarians and vegans**, and anyone eating very little animal protein.
- **People with stomach or intestinal surgery**, including bariatric surgery.
- **People on metformin** for diabetes over several years.
- **People on omeprazole** or similar medication long term.

If two or more of these describe you, testing once a year is reasonable.

Because if your fatigue is not from B12, injections will not lift it, and meanwhile the real cause goes untreated.

## And if it really is low?

Treatment depends on the cause. If the problem is diet, an oral supplement is sometimes enough. **If the problem is absorption, the injectable route makes sense**, because it bypasses the intestine.
